Abstract

A ground robot with one forward camera has to decide where it can drive in the next few metres, including the space it cannot see: behind furniture, and the 1.17 m of floor right in front of it that never appears in the image. Most monocular BEV work picks a map format first and then asks how well a network can predict it. We treat the representation itself as the design variable.

We generate aligned, amodal ground truth for seven candidate egocentric maps (occupancy, height-sliced occupancy, semantics, cost-to-go, clearance, reachability and a direction field) from privileged Habitat data, pair each with a fixed planner, and stress-test them in a whole-floor oracle and in a per-step closed loop on HM3D. On clean labels every representation is within a few points of the navmesh ceiling. Under controlled noise the planner matters as much as the map: binary occupancy collapses at 2% flipped cells, the clearance field stays robust to grown obstacles, and smooth fields survive their own noise only when a search-based planner consumes them. Single-frame visible labels lose 23–40 points for every representation, while fusing over time recovers most of the gap, the strongest evidence so far that the Stage 3 predictor must be amodal.

Problem setup

  • Robot: cylinder, radius 0.2 m, height 1.0 m; actions forward 0.25 m and turn 10°.
  • Sensor at test time: one RGB camera, 0.88 m high, 90° HFOV, no pitch, plus a point goal from odometry.
  • Local map: 80×80 egocentric grid at 5 cm, 4 m ahead and ±2 m sideways, robot at the bottom centre.
  • Success: within 0.2 m of the goal in at most 500 steps. Simulation only (Habitat, HM3D), static single-floor episodes.

We want three things:

  1. the representation whose planner degrades slowest when its map is wrong in the way a network would get it wrong;
  2. a predictor that outputs it from a single image, including occluded cells and the blind zone;
  3. an evaluation that measures map error and closed-loop navigation together, so the oracle gap (the cost of perception) is separated from the cost of the representation or planner.

Seven candidate representations

One pipeline turns depth, pose, semantics and a robot-sized navmesh into per-frame labels. Depth is back-projected and binned into height bands (floor [−0.15, 0.10) m, body [0.10, 1.0) m, overhead [1.0, 2.0) m). Visible labels use the current frame only. Amodal labels crop a per-floor map fused from 2,000–3,000 views, so they also cover occluded space and fill 83–90% of the blind zone.

"Visible depth" back-projects the camera's own depth image, the input a monocular network would replace; "noisy" scales each image by N(1, 0.05) and adds 2% per-pixel noise. Single-frame maps know only the visible ~30% of the cells the amodal label covers: in the visible region their mIoU is 0.91, so the loss is coverage, not accuracy. Exact visible depth fails by turning in place (all 56 failures run out of steps), a controller artefact of single-frame maps; fusing exact depth recovers 44 episodes and loses 3 (p < 10−9).

A label artefact we found: R7 snaps to 45°

On an empty floor, many staircase paths tie for shortest on the 8-connected grid, so R7 arrows snap towards multiples of 45°: mean error 17°, maximum 43°. The error follows the world grid while the label is egocentric, so it is label noise a network cannot explain. Grid planners are unaffected (oracle success 1.00), which is why the oracle missed it. Fast marching or any-angle paths will remove it, together with the length bias in R4 and R6.

Post mid-evaluation update · November 2026

Stage 3: learning the map from one RGB image

After the mid evaluation we trained a monocular predictor that turns one RGB frame into R1 (occupancy), R2 (height bands) and R5 (clearance) on the robot's 4 m egocentric grid, and plugged it into the same planners and the same minival episodes as every other source. We predict these three because a single image can actually determine them. R4, R6 and R7 depend on the goal and on rooms the camera cannot see, so the planners derive them from the predicted maps instead.

Data. 60 HM3D v0.2 training scenes (200 frames each) and 8 validation scenes (100 each), plus our earlier local scenes: 12,603 training and 795 validation frames, split by scene. The 10 minival scenes (00800–00809) are never trained on; they remain the test set. Training. AdamW (lr 3×10−4, weight decay 10−4), one-cycle schedule, 20 epochs, batch 16, horizontal flips and colour jitter, ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-50, depth-bin supervision from the depth sensor (training only). One Kaggle T4 per model.

Depth Anything 3 runs. Architectures C and D below swap the ResNet for Depth Anything 3 Small (DA3, Apache-2.0), fine-tuned at a tenth of the learning rate, with gradient clipping at 1.0; same data and losses. C trains 20 epochs with batch 16; D trains 12 epochs with batch 8, since every sample carries three images.

Architectures

A. Baseline Lift-Splat (11.9M parameters)

B. Ours: U-Net completion decoder (26.8M parameters) — changed blocks are what is new

Why these changes: lifting leaves occluded and out-of-view cells empty, and the baseline encoder only sees about 3.3 m around each cell, so it cannot reason about a whole room. The U-Net sees the whole grid, the visibility channels tell it which cells are guesses, and the loss weights push it to complete hidden space and get edges near obstacles right. Stride-8 features give four times as many rays.

C. Depth Anything 3 backbone (44.5M parameters) — changed blocks replace the ResNet-50 + FPN of B; everything after the features is B unchanged

DA3 predicts depth only up to an unknown scale, so its depth is not used as metric depth: it enters as two scale-free channels, and the depth-bin head learns the metric scale from the depth sensor during training. The encoder is fine-tuned at a tenth of the learning rate and runs in fp32 (fine-tuned in fp16 on the T4 its activations overflowed after four epochs); the depth head stays frozen.

D. Multi-view: DA3's cross-view attention over the robot's own recent views (44.7M parameters)

Two separate mechanisms use the extra views. Inside DA3, its cross-view attention lets every view's features see the others (the features we read out come from exactly those blocks). After the depth head, each context view is lifted into 3D and moved into the current grid with its pose, so cells the current camera cannot see get evidence from the earlier views. Poses come from odometry (exact in these runs).

Depth Anything 3 and multi-view context: a first look

The multi-view model (D) finished training just before this update. We ran a shorter minival check than for the other models, 400 frames for map quality (the first 40 of each minival scene) and 50 closed-loop episodes (the first 5 of each scene). Context in closed loop: the robot's own earlier frames from the current episode.

0.618 vs 0.590

Context views make better maps. With 2 extra views, R1 mIoU rises by 0.028 over the U-Net and over single-view DA3, on the same 400 frames: +0.028 behind objects, +0.046 outside the field of view, and R2 0.428 vs 0.399. On the validation scenes D reached 0.627 (C 0.594, B 0.592).

0.587

The gain comes from the views, not the training. The same multi-view weights given no context views score 0.587, level with the single-view models. The extra input is doing the work.

0.590 vs 0.590

A stronger backbone alone does not move R1. DA3 with one image ties the ResNet U-Net on R1. It improves what the camera sees (R2 visible 0.608 vs 0.581 over all 998 frames) but not hidden space, which one image cannot show.

Next steps

The results above point one way: hidden space is where the maps lose most, and the only thing that has improved it so far is more views. So the next round scales the multi-view model, borrowing the training recipe of Plann3r (which fine-tunes the cross-view layers of VGGT on a query image plus eight map images).

Training now (two Kaggle T4s)

  • v3 recipe, all 67 scenes: up to 3 context views, a random 0–3 per batch so one model serves any amount of history; depth supervision on every view, not just the current one; DA3's patch embedding and per-view blocks frozen, only its cross-view blocks trained (7.1M of its 22M encoder weights); an exponential moving average of the weights for evaluation; weight decay 0.05 on the backbone; and a fix that gives every data-loader worker its own augmentation stream.
  • Data-scaling point: the same recipe on 30 of the 67 scenes with the same number of steps. If 30 → 67 scenes still helps, the curve has not flattened, and more HM3D scenes are the next lever.

Then

  • Full minival for D and v3 (998 frames, 200 episodes) and with noisy odometry, since context poses then come from drift-prone odometry.
  • Whole-floor scans on all ten minival scenes, to test whether the per-frame gain of context survives fusion (on 00801 it does not: 0.728 vs 0.744).
  • Trajectory training data: context views from the robot's own driven trail rather than random nearby poses, so that training matches deployment.
  • Ablations that separate the two uses of the extra views: cross-view attention only, context splatting only, and the number of views.
  • Controller dither guard: every failure of every learned model is still a step-budget timeout.
  • With more compute: hundreds of HM3D scenes, DA3-Base, 4–8 views and three seeds per row.

One viewer for everything

A browser-based viewer drives the robot live in any scene (WASD), shows every representation from every source (ground truth, LiDAR, pseudo-LiDAR, noisy, fused, model), plans on the whole floor or drives the closed loop, and browses generated frames and result files.

Next steps (at the mid submission)

Label fixes before training

  • Fast marching or any-angle paths for R4, R6 and R7 (removes the 45° snapping and the length bias).
  • Clip or log-scale the R6 and R4 regression targets; several goals per frame for goal-conditioned heads.
  • One occupancy threshold rule for visible and amodal labels.

Planner fixes

  • Pick the local R4 target among cells connected to the robot inside the crop.
  • A dither guard that catches heading flips over 25° (the turn-in-place failures).
  • Symmetric log-odds when fusing predicted maps.

Stage 3 and 4

  • Monocular predictor: a Lift-Splat-style network with indoor depth range, height-banded splatting and a pretrained backbone (ResNet-50 / DINOv2), trained on HM3D scenes split by scene.
  • Fusion and ablations: odometry-based fusion of predictions; visible vs. amodal supervision; single-frame vs. accumulated.
